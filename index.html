    Iran effectively loses the Strait of Hormuz — and now its biggest Gulf trading partner — as the UAE severs all ties after fresh missile fire›

    Independent vessel tracking puts Hormuz transits at roughly six inbound tankers this week — thirteen total crossings including outbound, plus two dark tankers — against a pre-crisis baseline of about 73 ships a day, with the waterway still effectively closed to commercial shipping. The crisis deepened sharply on Aug 19, when the UAE's Foreign Ministry announced it is suspending all trade, commercial and financial dealings with Iran "until further notice," a day after detecting two ballistic missiles fired toward its territory, one of which fell inside UAE waters. The roughly $28 billion trade relationship had made the UAE one of Iran's most important economic lifelines, supplying more than 30% of its imports; the embargo lands on an economy the IMF already forecasts will contract 5.4% this year amid near-70% inflation. Iran's foreign ministry says Hormuz reopening conditions don't exist "as long as the U.S. naval blockade continues," and the 60-day US-Iran memorandum lapsed Aug 17 with no extension. US forces have redirected 64 vessels, disabled three and boarded two as of Aug 17, per United Against Nuclear Iran's tracker, while roughly 520 commercial vessels remain backed up in the Gulf. War-risk premiums have climbed to 7.5-10% of hull value, up from a pre-crisis 0.25% — roughly $21 million on a single $210 million VLCC transit. Brent crude climbed to $93.01 a barrel Thursday, its fifth consecutive daily rise.
