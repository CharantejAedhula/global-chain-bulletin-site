    Trump and Iran trade blame as Hormuz ship traffic sits near a three-month low and a Houthi strike kills six in the Red Sea›

    Washington keeps describing a Strait of Hormuz reopening as within reach — the narrative claim. The ships say otherwise. Kpler data show the five-day average of vessel transits fell to around 13 on Tuesday, near a three-month low, after traffic dropped to just six vessels on Monday against a 10-day average of about 11; the pre-war norm was 130-140 ships a day, and the independent straits.live tracker has logged the strait as effectively closed to commercial shipping for more than five months. Iran said Tuesday it will not agree to fully open the strait until the United States meets its demands, and President Trump used the same day to blast Tehran as hopes for a deal dim. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright says oil exports through the strait have still reached a seven-day average of nearly 9 million barrels a day, evidence that flows are being managed around the bottleneck rather than through an open lane. War-risk insurance keeps pricing the same reality: premiums for Gulf transits remain at 3% to 10% of a vessel's hull value, up from about 0.25% before the war. The picture worsened elsewhere in the region on the same news cycle — a suspected Houthi strike on the Egyptian-owned, Tanzania-flagged cargo ship Tihamah in the Bab-el-Mandeb strait killed six people on Tuesday, the first confirmed shipping deaths from the Houthi campaign in more than a year. Oman and Iran have separately agreed on shipping-lane coordinates and a 60-day interim mine-clearance arrangement, but that plan has not yet moved a single one of these numbers — a reminder that an agreed map and an open strait remain two different things.
